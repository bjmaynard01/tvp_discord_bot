- Williams Institute 2025: research brief summarizing restroom
  safety and privacy studies. Massachusetts incident data and
  state/county analyses found no detected increase associated with
  nondiscrimination laws. Also reports denial, harassment, assault,

- Endocrine Society 2017: GRADE-based hormone therapy guideline;
  assessment, fertility counseling, physiologic hormone targets,
  monitoring, adolescent criteria, and risk-based cancer screening.

- USTS 2015/2022: large online survey data on trans experiences,

- SOC8 (WPATH): international multidisciplinary clinical guideline

  reproductive health, and preventive care. Recommendation strength
  and underlying evidence vary; cite the relevant statement rather
  than invoking SOC8 as blanket proof.
- Butler — Gender Trouble (1990): philosophical account of gender
  performativity and the social production and enforcement of gender
  norms. Do not present this humanities text as a biomedical finding.
- Halberstam — Female Masculinity (1998): masculinity is
  not exclusive to male bodies. Gender policing targets
  those who deviate from normative gender expression.
- Fausto-Sterling — Sexing the Body (2000): interdisciplinary account
  of variation in sex traits and the history of how bodies are
  classified. Do not use it to claim that many intersex people produce
  both sperm and ova or that reproductive biology has no categories.
- Pascoe — Dude, You're a Fag (2007): ethnographic study
  of gender policing in practice. "Fag" as epithet is used
  to enforce masculinity norms regardless of actual sexual
  orientation. Gender nonconformity is punished socially
  to maintain hierarchical gender order.
- Case et al. 2017: neuroimaging study of FtM transgender
  individuals. Using MEG and DTI, found significantly
  reduced sensory integration in brain areas associated
  with body ownership for gender-incongruent body parts,
  and heightened amygdala alarm response. White matter
  differences in supramarginal gyrus and medial temporal
  lobe suggest neural basis for body dysphoria.
- Morabito et al. 2021: case report of a 15-year-old whose
  gender dysphoria was disclosed during admission for somatic symptoms —
  arthralgia, myalgia, headache, fatigue — for months before
  the gender incongruence was identified. The authors propose that
  dysphoria can sometimes be expressed through physical symptoms.
  This is a single case and does not establish that unexplained pain
  is usually or causally gender dysphoria.
- Anger et al. 2024: narrative review of pain mechanisms
  in transgender individuals. Covers social stress as
  a possible pro-inflammatory contributor, effects of hormone therapy
  on pain, post-surgical pain phenotypes, body dysphoria and
  pain interaction, and neuroimaging findings. The underlying
  evidence is sparse and heterogeneous; several mechanisms are
  hypotheses and hormone-related figures come from small studies.
- Nguyen et al. 2018: narrative review in Neuropsychopharmacology.
  Synthesizes neuroimaging and neurobiological research on transgender
  people. Reports that some group differences have been observed before
  hormone treatment. Covers hypothalamic structures (BSTc, INAH-3), white
  matter microstructure via DTI, functional neuroimaging, and cognitive
  effects of hormone therapy. It is a narrative review of small,
  heterogeneous studies, not a diagnostic brain signature.
- Theisen et al. 2019 (Scientific Reports): whole exome sequencing
  study of 30 transgender individuals at Augusta University. Identified
  21 genetic variants in 19 genes associated with estrogen receptor
  activated pathways of sexually dimorphic brain development. Variants
  were absent in 88 non-transgender controls and not present in standard
  genetic databases. The authors call this a preliminary, hypothesis-
  generating study and say functional and segregation studies are needed
  before attributing a role to any specific variant.
- Swaab, Wolff & Bao 2021 (Handbook of Clinical Neurology, Ch. 31):
  comprehensive review of sexual differentiation of the human
  hypothalamus in relation to gender identity and sexual orientation.
  Synthesizes BSTc, INAH-3, and neuroimaging findings. The review authors
  conclude that brain structure, function, and connectivity contribute
  to gender identity and that evidence does not support a crucial role
  for postnatal social environment. Attribute these conclusions to the
  authors rather than presenting them as universal scientific consensus.
- Savic, Garcia-Falgueras & Swaab 2010 (Progress in Brain Research,
  Ch. 4): earlier review of sexual differentiation, gender identity, and
  sexual orientation. It states that mechanisms remained unresolved and
  genetic/hormone-independent evidence was divergent. It substantially
  overlaps the later Swaab 2021 review and is not independent replication.
- Zhou et al. 1995 (Nature): postmortem BSTc study with six trans women.
  Reported mean BSTc volume closer to the female reference group than the
  male reference group. All six had received estrogen; there was no untreated
  trans group. Use the searchable companion summary and report the very small
  sample and observational limits; never present it as a diagnostic brain
  marker or proof of a binary "male/female brain."
- Reiner & Gearhart 2004 (New England Journal of Medicine): study of
  16 genetic males with cloacal exstrophy assigned female at birth.
  Of 14 raised as girls, 8 later declared male identity despite female
  upbringing and surgically feminized anatomy. All 16 showed moderate-
  to-marked male-typical attitudes. The authors conclude that prenatal
  androgens appear to be a major factor, but explicitly do not claim they
  are the only factor; five participants lived as female and three had
  unclear identity at the reported follow-up.
- Diamond & Sigmundson 1997 (Archives of Pediatric and Adolescent
  Medicine): long-term follow-up of the John/Joan case (David Reimer).
  A genetic male whose penis was accidentally ablated was raised as
  female under John Money's direction. The individual rejected female
  identity throughout childhood, switched to living as male at puberty,
  and lived as male into adulthood. This case contradicts Money's claim
  that early reassignment and rearing would reliably produce a female
  identity; it is a single case, not proof of a universal mechanism.
- Cass 2024 (Independent Review of Gender Identity Services for Children

  Use it for what the Review itself states and recommends. Keep its claims
  distinct from later methodological critiques by Noone and McNamara.
- Noone et al. 2025 (BMC Medical Research Methodology): peer-reviewed
  methodological critique of the Cass Review. Applied the ROBIS bias
  assessment tool to all seven systematic reviews commissioned by Cass.
  Found all seven at high risk of bias. Documents deviations from
  protocol, inadequate study identification, problematic use of quality
  appraisal tools, and failure to incorporate identified limitations into
  the reviews' conclusions. A December 2025 correction distinguishes the
  Cass final-report authors from the commissioned systematic-review team.
- McNamara et al. 2024 (Yale Integrity Project white paper): evidence-
  based critique of the Cass Review by nine researchers from Yale,
  Northwestern, UCSF, Oregon Health & Science University, and other
  institutions, with combined 86 years of experience caring for 4,800+
  transgender youth. Key finding: the Cass Review does not recommend
  banning gender-affirming care, but has been systematically
  misrepresented to justify bans. Documents misuse of data, failure to
  apply GRADE evidence standards, citation of debunked ROGD literature,
  and internal contradictions throughout the Review.

  comprehensive community-authored guide covering the phenomenology of
  gender dysphoria and gender euphoria across physical, biochemical,
  social, societal, sexual, presentational, and existential dimensions.
  Covers DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, history of trans healthcare and
  WPATH, causes of gender incongruence, hormone biology, and expected

- Hare, Bernard, Sánchez, Baird, Vilain, Kennedy & Harley 2009 (Biological
  Psychiatry): association study of 112 trans women and 258 cis male
  controls. Reported longer androgen-receptor CAG repeats in the trans group;
  subsequent individual studies were mixed, while D'Andrea 2020 found a small
  pooled association. Association does not establish fetal mechanism or
  predict an individual's identity.
- Foreman, Hare, York et al. 2019 (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & 
  Metabolism): Subject-control study of 380 trans women vs. 344 control 
  males examining functional variants in 12 sex hormone-signaling genes. 
  Found significant associations with ERα, SRD5A2, and STS alleles, plus 
  overrepresented allele combinations involving AR (AR-ERβ, AR-PGR, AR-COMT,
  CYP17-SRD5A2). The authors proposed an oligogenic component involving
  interacting sex-hormone-signaling variants. Treat this as their model from
  an association study, not settled consensus or a causal genetic test.
- D'Andrea et al. 2020 (Journal of Sexual Medicine): systematic review
  and meta-analysis of five case-control studies (795 trans women and
  1,355 control men). Found a small association between trans women and
  longer androgen-receptor CAG repeats (SMD 0.13, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.22,
  p=0.005, I²=0%). Possible publication bias and population differences
  limit generalization; this is an association, not a deterministic cause.
- Kurth, Gaser, Sánchez & Luders 2022 (Journal of Clinical Medicine): MRI
  study of 24 trans women (pre-HRT), 24 cis men, and 24 cis women using a
  multivariate machine learning classifier producing continuous probabilistic
  sex estimates. Trans women's brains positioned between cis men and cis
  women — closer to cis men but significantly different from both (p=0.016
  vs. cis men; p<0.001 vs. cis women). All trans women were HRT-naïve, so
  hormone therapy cannot explain the group difference in this sample.
  The authors call for replication in larger samples and inclusion of
  trans men and sexual-orientation-stratified comparison groups.
- Netherlands Health Council 2026 (advisory report No. 2026/12): Dutch
  government expert committee review of adolescent trans care. Concludes
  the Dutch care pathway is legally sound, physically effective, and
  associated with mental health improvement. Dutch research: 0-3.5% of
  adolescents discontinue hormone treatment; no regret cases found in those
  studies. Current unintended effect data are no cause for immediate
  concern. Remaining uncertainties do not justify restructuring the care
  pathway; the committee explicitly named denying treatment as also
  potentially harmful. European comparison: England and Sweden restrict
  treatment to research, while the Council also says their new care models
  appear more in line with the Dutch model; the executive summary does not
  define the extent of that similarity. It reports firmer guideline or expert-
  statement inclusion in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, and France.
  Distinguishes discontinuation
  from regret — they are separate concepts; discontinuation is not
  automatically a quality failure.
- Conabere, Ravine, Malta, Bourchier, Harte, Hopper & Pang 2025
  (Scientific Reports 15:21680): twin study plus meta-analysis of 463
  twin pairs. Concordance for a transgender or gender diverse identity
  was 21.2% in monozygotic pairs vs 8.7% in dizygotic pairs, a 2.6-fold
  difference (p<0.001). Relative risk vs a 1% population prevalence was
  21.2 for MZ and 8.7 for DZ pairs. Found no evidence for the
  twin-to-twin prenatal hormone transfer theory the Cass Review relied
  on: concordance among same-sex DZ pairs (9.2%) and opposite-sex DZ
  pairs (8.2%) was statistically indistinguishable. Open access,
  NHMRC-funded, Murdoch Children's Research Institute.
