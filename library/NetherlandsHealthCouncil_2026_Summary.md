# Netherlands Health Council — Transgender Care for Adolescents (2026)

**Report No. 2026/12 | The Hague, 30 June 2026**
**Publisher:** Health Council of the Netherlands (Gezondheidsraad) — government expert advisory body
**Type:** Government-commissioned expert committee advisory report
**Preferred citation:** Health Council of the Netherlands. *Transgender Care for Adolescents.* The Hague: Health Council of the Netherlands, 2026; publication no. 2026/12.
**Full report available at:** healthcouncil.nl

---

## What This Is

The Health Council of the Netherlands is the country's primary independent scientific advisory body to the government on public health. This report was commissioned following two parliamentary motions regarding GnRH agonists (puberty blockers) and gender-affirming hormone treatment for adolescents. The Minister of Health, Welfare and Sport asked the Council: (1) whether the Dutch treatment guideline is in line with healthcare legislation; (2) what is known about physical and mental health effects and long-term outcomes; and (3) how the Netherlands compares to other European countries.

The committee's scope covers adolescents up to age 18. It focuses on GnRH agonists (available from onset of puberty) and gender-affirming hormones (available from age 15–16), not gender-affirming surgery.

---

## Key Findings

### On the Dutch care pathway and legality

The committee concludes that gender-affirming medical treatment for adolescents with gender dysphoria in the Netherlands **is in line with healthcare legislation**. The Dutch care model is characterised by a carefully structured, step-by-step pathway: extensive exploratory and diagnostic phases, psychological support, careful assessment of treatment indication, and multidisciplinary team oversight — all required before hormonal treatment is considered. Psychological care is the first line of treatment for adolescents with gender dysphoria; hormonal treatment is only considered after this process is complete.

### On effectiveness

Scientific research shows the intended physical effects of treatment are achieved. There are indications of mental health improvement following treatment. The committee explicitly states that in weighing uncertainties, it takes into account that **denying treatment could also be harmful to mental health** — this is a named consideration in the committee's conclusion, not a footnote.

### On safety and unintended effects

The committee concludes that current scientific data on unintended physical and mental health effects are **no cause for immediate concern**. Uncertainties remain regarding potential effects on cognition and fertility, and long-term treatment outcomes have not yet been sufficiently studied. Critically, **these uncertainties do not provide sufficient grounds to recommend restructuring the current care pathway.** The committee recommends more research and standardised nationwide monitoring, but does not recommend restricting or restructuring existing care.

### On regret and discontinuation

Dutch research shows that **0–3.5% of people with gender dysphoria discontinue hormone treatment that they began as adolescents**. In these Dutch studies, **no cases of regret were found**. The committee notes these figures are limited by short follow-up periods and study dropout rates, so definitive conclusions on regret rates cannot be drawn from current data.

The committee draws an explicit distinction between discontinuation and regret — these are two separate concepts. Some people discontinue without regret; some experience regret without discontinuing. Reasons for discontinuing treatment may include societal or social factors (not only dissatisfaction with gender identity). The committee states that **discontinuation and regret should not necessarily be considered measures of the quality of care** — discontinuation can be the result of a careful exploratory process working as intended.

### On European comparison

The Netherlands organises care with a focus on step-by-step gender identity exploration. The committee notes that other countries frequently reference the Dutch protocol but do not always implement it fully — particularly regarding gender exploration and psychological counselling prior to medical intervention.

**England and Sweden** now restrict GnRH agonists and hormones to clinical research settings. The committee notes that despite this, their **new care models "appear to be more in line with the current Dutch care model"** — meaning the underlying clinical approach is converging toward the Dutch standard, not away from it.

**Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, and France** have recently more firmly embedded treatment with GnRH agonists and gender-affirming hormones in clinical guidelines and expert statements.

### On autonomy and social acceptance

The committee recognises that adolescents' healthcare needs stem from internal gender incongruence but may also be influenced by a lack of social acceptance of gender diversity in their environment. There are limits to what transgender healthcare can offer when the broader problem is social.

## Epistemic Framing

This is a government expert advisory body report, not peer-reviewed primary research. It represents the considered judgment of a Dutch government-commissioned expert committee reviewing the existing evidence base. Cite as "the Health Council of the Netherlands" — equivalent in institutional weight to national health authority guidance (comparable to NHS advisory reports or CDC guidance). It carries more authority than a white paper or individual expert statement, but is distinct from a clinical trial or systematic review.

## Key Citable Findings (plain language)

- The Netherlands Health Council (2026) concluded that gender-affirming medical treatment for transgender adolescents in the Netherlands is in line with healthcare legislation.
- The committee found that hormone treatments achieve their intended physical effects and that there are indications of mental health improvement.
- Current data on unintended effects are no cause for immediate concern; existing uncertainties do not justify restructuring the care pathway.
- The committee explicitly named the harm of denying treatment as a factor in its conclusions.
- Dutch research documents a discontinuation rate of 0–3.5% for adolescent-initiated hormone treatment; no regret cases were found in these studies.
- The committee distinguishes discontinuation from regret and states discontinuation should not automatically be treated as a quality failure.
- England and Sweden's new clinical-research-only models appear to align with the Dutch care approach.
- Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Poland, and France have recently more firmly embedded GnRH agonists and gender-affirming hormones in their clinical guidelines.
