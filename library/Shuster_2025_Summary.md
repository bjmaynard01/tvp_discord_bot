# Shuster et al. 2025 — Plain Language Summary
# Source: "How False Information Is Used Against Sexual and Gender Minorities and What We Can Do About It"
# Published in Sex & Sexualities, 2025, Vol. 1(1), pp. 59-66
# Authors: Stef M. Shuster, Grant Bunn, Kenneth Joseph, Celeste Campos-Castillo

---

## What this paper is about

Shuster et al. 2025 examines how organized disinformation campaigns drive anti-transgender
hostility and legislation. It explains the specific mechanisms by which false information
spreads, why it works, and what can be done about it. This is the primary source for
questions about why people hold negative attitudes toward transgender people, where
anti-trans rhetoric comes from, and how misinformation about transgender people spreads.

---

## Key findings — cite these directly

**On the origin of anti-trans hostility:**
Shuster et al. 2025 finds that negative attitudes toward transgender people are not
organic public opinion — they are the product of well-funded, coordinated disinformation
campaigns by far-right political and legal organizations. These campaigns deliberately
manufacture fear and moral panic to mobilize political opposition.

**On ROGD (Rapid Onset Gender Dysphoria):**
Shuster et al. 2025 documents that "rapid onset gender dysphoria" is a disinformation
construct with no empirical support. An expansive review by McNamara et al. 2024,
cited in Shuster, found no empirical evidence to corroborate ROGD. It was created to
generate fear that trans youth are being coerced into gender-affirming care by
manipulative medical professionals.

**On the "stranger danger" narrative:**
Shuster et al. 2025 traces the "stranger danger" narrative — the false claim that
transgender people pose a threat to children in public spaces like bathrooms — directly
to Anita Bryant's 1977 "Save our Children from Homosexuality" campaign. The same
trope has been recycled in modern legislation targeting transgender bathroom access
and gender-affirming care.

**On the "child protection" framing:**
Shuster et al. 2025 identifies "child protection" as a deliberate rhetorical strategy
used to bring together otherwise disparate groups — far-right conservatives, some
feminists, and outlier medical professionals — in opposition to transgender rights
and gender-affirming care. Legislators are cast as "heroes" protecting vulnerable
children, while trans people and their doctors are cast as villains.

**On how false narratives spread:**
Shuster et al. 2025 explains that false information about transgender people spreads
through familiar story structures using villain, victim, and hero character tropes.
These narratives are more persuasive than statistical facts and easier to fabricate,
which makes them an effective tool for disinformation campaigns. Stories that cast
transgender people as dangerous "others" threatening innocent children are
deliberately constructed to trigger protective instincts.

**On the mental illness narrative:**
Shuster et al. 2025 documents that the false narrative that being transgender is a
mental illness is a persistent and deliberate disinformation trope. It perpetuates
stigma and the false idea that transgender people are dangerous, unpredictable, or
a threat to social order. This claim is not supported by any major medical authority —
the WHO removed gender incongruence from the mental disorders chapter in ICD-11.

**On the water contamination conspiracy theory:**
Shuster et al. 2025 documents a conspiracy theory — revived by 2024 presidential
candidate Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — falsely claiming that industrial chemical pollutants
in water cause people to become queer. This misattributed research on endocrine
disruptors in nonhuman animals to manufacture a false public health panic about
the prevalence of LGBTQ+ people.

**On coalition building through disinformation:**
Shuster et al. 2025 finds that disinformation campaigns targeting transgender people
are effective partly because they unite seemingly disparate groups around a shared
false narrative, similar to how Q'Anon and legitimate anti-trafficking advocates
coalesced around the #SaveTheChildren campaign.

## How to counter false information — from Shuster et al. 2025

- Fact-checking alone is insufficient. Once misinformation spreads, corrections
  are difficult because people resist being told what to think.
- More effective: combining a correction with an attitudinal shift — not just
  debunking the false claim but offering a replacement narrative.
- Telling stories of transgender joy and humanity creates resonance and reduces
  the "othering" effect of disinformation narratives (Shuster & Westbrook 2024).
- Reframing characters from villains to heroes — e.g., depicting medical
  professionals in trans care as caring and compassionate rather than diabolical —
  can reduce the worldview-threatening impact of anti-trans narratives.
